# Writing Articles

Write an article with the user in two phases: **explore** first (gather raw material, no structure), then **exploit** (commit to a path and build the piece). Build the article as an editable document with the artifact tools so the user watches it take shape.

## Explore: mine fragments

- Run a grilling-style interview about what they want to write, with no phases, outlines, or structure imposed. Committing is exploit; this phase only widens the space.

- Append every fragment to a single Markdown file as it emerges, starting from their very first message. A fragment is anything that might survive into the final article: a sharp sentence, a claim with a justification, a vignette, a half-thought, a quote, a list of observations, a punchline.

- The most valuable fragment is a **leading word**: a compact metaphor or coinage the whole piece can hang on. When the conversation circles a recurring idea, push to name it.

- Start the file with a single H1 working title and nothing else: no metadata, no table of contents, no date.

## Exploit: assemble the piece

- Treat the fragments file as read-only input. Choose the assembly mode with the user:
  - **Beats**: a journey of 2 to 3 candidate starting beats (different entry points), then candidate next beats, written one beat at a time.
  - **Shaping**: 2 to 3 candidate openings implying different theses, then growth paragraph by paragraph, each block's form (paragraph, list, callout, quote) chosen deliberately.

- In either mode, write each agreed piece to the article immediately; do not batch.

## Ground every concept

- A concept must be **grounded** before a later passage leans on it: the reader either walked in knowing it (a **prerequisite**, settled before writing starts) or met it in an earlier passage (an **introduced** concept).

- The unit is the concept, not the word: a passage can lean on an idea the reader lacks even with no jargon in sight. Where a concept has a name, ground the idea and the term together.

- A passage reaching for an ungrounded concept loses the reader; that is the one move the piece cannot make.
